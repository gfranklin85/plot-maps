# I Want

### The thesis behind a marketplace that runs the right direction

---

## Where this came from

It started in real estate, with one stubborn observation: the buyer is the prime mover. No ready buyer, no transaction — no commission, no loan, no listing that means anything. Demand is the thing that creates all the motion. And yet the whole industry was built to treat the buyer as the supplicant: the one who chases, qualifies, gets pre-approved, competes against other buyers, and absorbs the fees. The most valuable person in the room was dressed up as the one asking for a favor.

The fix wasn't to remove competition. Competition is the engine — that's the free market doing its job. The fix was to point the competition the right direction. Instead of the buyer applying outward to lender after lender, hat in hand, the buyer stands still and the market applies inward to *them*.

That's the inversion. And once you see it in real estate, you can't unsee it anywhere. Because it isn't a real estate idea. It's a description of how every market actually works underneath the costume.

---

## What buying even is

Walk it back. What happens before anyone buys anything? They want something. Not "need" — want. Need is the abstraction; want is where the specifics live.

"I'm hungry" is a need, and it's useless to anyone trying to help. "I want a cheeseburger under six bucks near downtown, right now" is a want — and it's instantly answerable. It carries the thing itself, the budget, the constraints, the urgency, all in one breath. "I want a laptop, around $200, that runs Ableton without choking and survives my kid" — the underlying need is just *a computer*, generic and worthless as a signal. The want has the whole brief baked in.

So "I want" isn't a slogan. It's the best intake format that exists. Need tells you the category. Want hands you the spec. Every want is a person doing the seller's research for them, for free, and saying it out loud.

That's why the whole thing starts with two fixed words. You don't fill out a form. You don't get profiled. You finish a sentence: **I want ___.**

## The crowd was always the product

Here's the part most people get backwards — including most people who build marketplaces.

The crowd was never the audience for the vendors. The crowd was the product. People never came to the market for the vendors; the vendors came to the market for the people. The agora, the medieval market town, the shopping mall, Amazon, Google — every one of them is the same machine: a place that concentrates demand and then charges vendors for proximity to it. The anchor store gets cheap rent *because* it pulls the crowd. The little shops pay a premium to sit in the gravity it creates.

Demand is the asset. It always was. We just got used to the vendors owning the building.

A site that starts with "I want" doesn't invent that arrangement — it just stops hiding it. It hands the gravity back to the people who were generating it the whole time.

---

## Your front door, not their destination

Every place you'd compare this to — Amazon, the marketplace apps, the store — is a destination. You leave your own ground and travel to theirs: their search box, their shelf logic, their idea of what's relevant, their merchandising. And the quiet rule of all commerce is that whoever travels arrives in the weaker position. You show up framed by what they wanted you to see.

This runs the travel the other way. The want is posted from where you already stand, in your own words, and the people who can answer cross the distance to you. It isn't shopping made convenient. It's your front door — your ordering window at home — and the standing is literally spatial: you never leave, so you never arrive on anyone's terms but your own.

It's also clean by structure, which is a different thing than well-moderated. The marketplaces are dirty because *you* do the sifting — scroll, filter, dismiss the junk, wade through the irrelevant to maybe find the one you'll take. The labor of relevance is shoved onto the buyer. Here you state the want once and the sifting happens on the answerers' side, before they ever reach you. You don't pan the river for gold. You say bring me gold, and the river sorts itself.

## Declared demand kills the guessing industry

Today, demand is almost never declared. It's inferred.

The entire modern ad economy is a multi-hundred-billion-dollar apparatus built to *guess* what you want. Tracking, profiling, retargeting, the ads that follow you from site to site — that whole surveillance layer exists for one reason: nobody can just see your want, so they reconstruct it from your exhaust. They spy on you because you never said it.

Say it, and there is nothing left to surveil.

This is the structural blow, and it's bigger than "another marketplace." A want stated in the first person makes the entire inference machine pointless. No tracking required when the person simply tells you. And here's the deeper cut: vendors don't actually want to serve declared demand. They want to *manufacture* it — that's what advertising is, the business of creating wants that weren't there. A place where people just say what they already want serves real demand instead of inventing it. That's anti-advertising as an economic principle. Which is exactly why it would feel like the ground moving.

## The three roles

A want sits down in the middle of a room. Three kinds of people are in that room.

**The one who wants.** They post the sentence and nothing else. They don't justify it, qualify for it, or apologize for it. They announce demand and let the world organize around it.

**The ones who can bring it.** They present offerings — and they compete *because they can see each other*. But the competition isn't a contest the platform scores and awards. There's no correct answer to compute. One brings a lower price, another better terms, another a longer warranty, another a brand the buyer has trusted his whole life. They're all competing for one thing: his attention, and his yes. The platform doesn't rank them, doesn't crown a winner, doesn't decide which offering "fits" best — because the moment it picks, it has quietly taken the throne back from the buyer. A reverse auction picks for you: lowest number wins, you're just the wallet. Even a fit score picks for you: the algorithm decides what answers your sentence. Both put the buyer back underneath. This doesn't. Its only job is to raise him up high enough to see every offering clearly. The choosing is his — and his reasons are his own. He might take the pricier one, or the older brand, or the seller who just seemed straight, and none of it has to make sense to anyone but him.

**The ones with nothing to sell.** They comment. They can't pitch, which is exactly what makes them trustworthy. "That machine's graphics will stutter once you stack plugins." "That chassis is plastic — look at the metal one if it has to survive a kid." Free, honest, no angle. Every comment makes every offer in the thread more honest, because someone in the room benefits from the truth instead of from the sale.

Offerings compete for your yes. Comments can't sell. You're raised above all of it, and you choose.

---

## Plurality is the protection

The load-bearing word in all of this is *else*. You don't just want the right provider to bring it — you want everyone capable of bringing it to know that somebody else might bring it first.

If only one vendor saw your want, you'd be cornered — take their offering or take nothing, on their terms. The protection was never privacy. The protection is plurality — a whole field of capable people all seeing the same want, every one of them an option you can take or pass on. You're never stuck with a single answer, so no single seller can hold you. Your want isn't a wish you whispered to one of them. It's an open invitation, and the power in it is that *you* decide who gets to be the one who answered.

---

## A standing want is a forecast

A one-off post is good. A standing want is worth more.

"I'm in the market for a reliable used truck under $18k, and I will be for the next six months" isn't a whim — it's a forecast. It's reliable demand a provider can plan around. It's the precise thing vendors currently spend fortunes trying to predict from your browsing history. So give everyone a page that just *says it*. Not a profile of who you are — a profile of what you're in the market for. The status layer of the internet is people performing what they have. This is the missing layer: people stating what they want, on purpose, so the market can find them instead of stalking them.

---

## No scoreboard, anywhere

This is the single rule that holds the whole thing together, and it's the one a profit-first copy can never keep.

A scoreboard is any mechanism that ranks, scores, or awards — and every one of them quietly hands the throne back to the platform. An ad auction is a scoreboard that decides who you see. A fit score is a scoreboard that decides what answers your sentence. An upvote count is a scoreboard that decides whose voice carries. Each one re-centers the system as the thing that judges, and re-seats the buyer underneath it.

So the discipline is to refuse all of them, at every layer. No ranked offerings — you choose. No computed best answer — you choose. And no scored comments — because the instant a comment can earn points or a follower count, people stop talking *to the buyer* and start performing *for the gallery*, and the help curdles into the same outrage-shaped discourse you were escaping. Address comments to the originator of the thread, never to the room. Strip the gallery and there's no one to perform for, so the only reason left to comment is to actually help.

That's why the genuineness isn't a hope pinned on good users. It's the residue of refusing, at every layer, to build the thing that would have corrupted it. And it's why care-first isn't a slogan — it's structural. The moment a builder wants the cut more than the dynamic, they reach for a scoreboard by reflex, and the building reverts to a mall. Anyone can copy the screen. Nobody who's profit-first can keep the rule.

## The only real friction

The technical build is easy. The one thing to get right is the posture — and there's a misread to clear, not a wound to manage.

Stating a want is not an admission of lack. That's the old frame's word, and it carries exactly the wrong charge. Lack is the supplicant's condition — the empty-handed one hoping to be filled. A want is the opposite of that. A king summoning a thing isn't confessing a deficiency; he's exercising authority. The want is a command issued from a position of standing: *bring me this.* The person who posts it is the originator of the thread, the one who holds the floor while everyone capable of answering looks at each other and then at him. There's no deficit in the room. There's a sovereign with a standing order.

The only friction is that a culture trained to perform having-it-all will, at first, misread a plainly stated want as neediness. That misread is expected, and it corrects itself — because command doesn't beg, and people can feel the difference. A want stated from standing reads as composure, not exposure. You don't argue someone out of the misconception; the posture itself does the converting. Say what you want, plainly, as the one directing the room to bring it, and the people who mistook it for weakness recalibrate the moment they see the market cross the floor toward you.

That's the same posture, one last time, generalized to everything a person could ever want:

**A want isn't a hole in you. It's a command from the head of the thread. Say it out loud.**
