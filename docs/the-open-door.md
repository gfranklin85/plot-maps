# The Open Door

*Why PlotMaps exists, told straight.*

---

## Prologue: a door that wouldn't open

I asked a woman at a desk for a copy of the data. Public records, mostly — the kind of information that describes who owns what, the raw material of the work I do every day. She looked at me and said, "Well, *you* can't."

Not "here's the fee." Not "here's the form." *You* can't. Because I hadn't paid the right dues, joined the right club, sworn to the right people. The information wasn't hers. It wasn't really the club's either. But the door was theirs to hold shut, and she held it shut, and she enjoyed it a little.

I've built this whole thing around that sentence. Not out of spite — out of the simple recognition that a door somebody holds shut for no reason other than that they *can* is a door that deserves to be walked around. This is the story of walking around it, and of the better rooms on the other side.

It's not a rant. The facts don't need me angry. They just need to be laid out where a normal person can see them, because most people never do — the machinery is kept behind a counter, and the person at the counter has been taught to call the machinery "the rules."

## Chapter 1 — I built tools before I hit the wall

I started as an agent, not a broker. And almost from the beginning I was building — client portals, QR codes on signs that pulled up the property, little deal rooms, ways to automate the boring parts. Not because anyone asked. Because the tools we were handed were bad, and I couldn't stand it.

Somewhere in there I found Google's photorealistic 3D world — the whole Earth, rendered, flyable. And I did the obvious thing nobody had done: I made it fly with a game controller. You could lift off your own street and soar to the pyramids, drop into a canyon, skim a coastline. It was the map, but it was *play*. It was wonder.

That could have been the whole company. A flight simulator of the real Earth is a genuinely rare thing to have built. But I was an agent, and I kept seeing the same faces underneath the wonder — the buyer priced out, the owner stuck, the family that couldn't find the door. So I pointed the tool at the work: fly the map, find the parcel, learn who owns it, reach them kindly, help them move. That's when it became PlotMaps.

And then I became a broker. And the wall I'd been leaning on my whole career turned around and looked at me.

## Chapter 2 — How the machine actually works

Let me describe the machine plainly, because almost nobody outside the trade has seen it described at all.

**The MLS is not a service. It's a gate.** The Multiple Listing Service presents itself as a helpful shared database of homes for sale. What it actually is: the single on-ramp to the entire buying public. Nearly every place a normal person looks for a home — the big portals everyone knows — is fed by the MLS. If your property isn't in the MLS, it is, for practical purposes, invisible. The MLS doesn't just *organize* the market. It **owns the entrance to it.**

And you cannot enter the entrance without paying. To put a listing where buyers can see it, you generally need to be a dues-paying member of the MLS, which generally means being a member of the trade associations, which means desk fees and a brokerage and a stack of tolls — each one small enough to seem reasonable, all of them together forming a fence around the simple act of telling the public your house is for sale.

**The associations dress their bylaws up as law.** This is the sleight-of-hand, and it's the part that took me years to see clearly. The National Association and its state affiliates write rules — private rules, club rules — and present them with the gravity of statute. Agents come to believe that things which are merely *association policy* are *the law*. They're not. A private organization cannot make law. But if everyone in the room believes the club's handbook is the legal code, the club has all the power of a government and none of the accountability. It's a magic trick: say it firmly enough, in the right building, and a preference becomes a commandment.

**When I became a broker, the machine made its demand explicit.** I was told, in effect, that my entire brokerage would have to be dues-paying members of the club — every agent, all in — or my brokerage would be relegated to a lesser status, a referral shell that can't really operate. Pay for all of us, or be pushed to the margin. That's not a marketplace. That's a protection arrangement with better letterhead.

I want to be careful here, because it would be easy to make this sound like a conspiracy of villains. It isn't. It's something more ordinary and more durable: an architecture *built to extract*, staffed by people who've been taught the extraction is just "how it's done." The woman at the desk wasn't evil. She was a gatekeeper who'd forgotten the gate was optional. The whole system runs on that forgetting.

You don't fight an architecture like that by arguing with the people inside it. You build a better one and stand on it.

## Chapter 3 — The buyer was left to fend for the most important part

Here's the piece that made me sure the problem was moral and not just annoying.

At the most important moment of the largest purchase of a person's life — the money, the financing, the terms — the buyer is very often *on their own.* There's a rule, RESPA, that keeps an agent from steering a client to a particular lender, and it exists for a good reason: it stops kickbacks, it stops quiet corruption. But it left a gap. In closing that one door to abuse, the system left the buyer standing in a hallway alone at the exact moment they most needed a team.

So what fills the gap? The lead-sellers fill it. The big portals discovered that the buyer themselves is the product — that you can charge professionals for *access* to that buyer, auction the human being to whichever lender or agent pays the most, and call it a service. The buyer thinks they're being helped. They're being sold. Every "get pre-qualified" button, every "connect with a lender," is often a turnstile with a price on the other side, and the price is the buyer's own attention, sold back to the people who want to win it.

I kept coming back to how *backwards* that is. The lender wants the buyer. The buyer needs the lender. Both parties want to meet. And an entire industry has inserted itself into the middle of that mutual wanting to charge a toll on the introduction.

## Chapter 4 — The geometry nobody rebuilt

I want to step back further than real estate, because the real problem is older and larger, and once you see it you can't unsee it.

Think about where we live and where we shop. People live in their homes. Long ago we all agreed, sensibly, that the marketplace doesn't belong at your front door — so commerce got pushed downtown, to the market square, to the shop on the corner. Fine. Reasonable.

But that arrangement quietly created a *power geometry*, and we've lived inside it so long we mistake it for nature. **To get what you want, you have to travel to their domain.** You leave your ground and enter theirs. And the moment you walk into someone else's shop, you are in the *asking* position. You're the visitor. You're on their turf. However gracious they are, the room belongs to them.

It compounds. You don't meet the market all at once — you meet it one merchant at a time, in sequence, each one working you one-on-one, each with the full advantage of their own ground and their own practice. Even if there were a food court of lenders under one roof, you'd still be walked through it and worked over booth by booth. And here's the cruel psychological part: the customer walks in already feeling smaller. They've never played this game. The merchant has played it ten thousand times. It's an away game against a team that's never lost at home — and even when the merchant is honest, even when the customer is sharp, the customer *still feels the disadvantage*, because the geometry manufactures it.

Now here's what almost nobody notices. That merchant went to enormous trouble — signed a lease, built the shop, hung the sign — for one reason: **to win your business.** *You are the prize.* But the arrangement hides that completely. The person who is being competed for walks in feeling like an intruder. The architecture inverts the truth of who needs whom.

And then the internet arrived. We were handed a blank canvas — a chance to build the market any shape we wanted, unbound from streets and downtowns and the tyranny of who-travels-to-whom. And what did we do? **We rebuilt the exact same geometry, in pixels.** You still "go to" the lender's website. You still fill out *their* form on *their* domain. You still approach the merchants one at a time, in the asking posture, at the away game. We had the one moment in history when the old physics didn't apply, and we reassembled the old inferiority out of habit.

So here is the thing I keep saying, the thing under everything I'm building: it did not have to be this way, and it does not have to stay this way. Picture it the honest way instead. Picture standing at your own door — your ground — and simply calling out what you want. And a flurry of merchants comes *to you*, to your ground, bringing their best, competing in the open for the privilege of your business. You host. They arrive. You choose.

Nobody built that surface. I'm building that surface.

## Chapter 5 — The Interconnector: the deal no one could see alone

Once you decide the market should come to the person, the biggest idea follows almost on its own.

Every real-estate tool ever made assumes a *search*. You, the shopper, scan a list of what already exists, and you shrink your dream down until it fits the inventory that happens to be for sale this week. That's the whole model: conform yourself to the listings.

I want the opposite. I want you to say what you actually want — the real thing, the acre near town you'd fix up yourself, the coast, the place you'd only ever move for — and I want the system to go *build the deal that gets you there.* Not find it. Build it.

Because most moves die pairwise. You have a house someone else would love, and you want a house someone else is sitting in, but your buyer and your seller aren't the same transaction, and no single swap closes, so everyone stays stuck. A one-for-one marketplace can only ever match coincidences, which is why home-swap sites are ghost towns.

The Interconnector doesn't hunt for coincidences. It holds what *everyone* wants and what *everyone* can actually carry — the dreams and the numbers, both sides — and it finds the **loop.** A wants what B has; B wants what C has; C wants what A has; the circle closes and three families all move, though no two of them ever directly matched. Add a fourth, a fifth. Fold in a person who just cashes out at the end and lets the chain complete. It's the shape of an NBA trade — nobody swaps straight across; a third team and the right pieces get folded in until every constraint is satisfied at once — except the trade is people's lives, and the office running it is automated, and the constraints include the honest arithmetic of what each household can pay.

No human broker can hold all of that in their head. No search box can see it. Only a system with the whole web in view can find the move that was invisible to every single person inside it. That's not a feature. That's a kind of sight nobody had before.

## Chapter 6 — The tools, given away

So what do we actually do differently. Concretely.

**Post your listing, free.** No dues, no club, no permission, no toll. You put your property on a map the public is already exploring, and it's seen. That single act — making the on-ramp free and open — quietly makes the gate optional. Not by fighting the gate. By building a wider road beside it and not charging anyone to drive on it.

**Write the documents in plain language.** The contracts and disclosures a normal person actually reads, not fine print engineered to be initialed and forgotten. A deal is honest when it's *legible.* If you have to be a professional to understand what you're signing, the document is doing a job other than informing you.

**Bring the whole market to the buyer.** This is the geometry corrected, made real. Instead of the buyer traveling booth to booth in the asking posture, the buyer — or the agent, on their client's behalf — puts their situation up once, on their own ground, and the lenders and the vendors come *to them* with their estimates, in the open, side by side. The buyer hosts. The professionals compete. And they compete on the merits, because there's no toll to buy your way to the top of the list — no one is paying to be the answer. This is the food court turned inside out: not you walking the booths, but the booths coming to your door and making their case in daylight.

And this last one is the part an agent hears and says *exactly*, because an agent has lived the other version — has watched a client scramble alone for financing at the worst possible moment. Now the agent can hand that client a surface where the entire market shows up to earn them. That's a thing no other tool lets an agent give.

Most of this is free. That's deliberate, and it's not charity. The free tools are the magnet. The value we're paid for is the honest transaction itself, done well, by a real brokerage that stands behind it. When your money comes from the move actually happening — and not from tolling the introductions, selling the leads, or renting the gate — you can afford to give the rest away. In fact you *must*, because giving it away is what fills the map that makes the whole thing work.

## Chapter 7 — Two movements, one escape

There are two things moving here at once, and it's worth seeing how they fit.

**The loud one is the storefront.** PlotMaps is the public face — the flyable Earth, the free listings, the buyer's platform, the want-map that connects people. It's the thing regular people will actually know by name. It draws the crowd, fills the map, and makes the market a place you'd *want* to visit even if you weren't buying anything.

**The quiet one is the deeper change, and it runs through the brokerage.** There is an enormous reservoir of licensed agents who simply stopped playing — not because they couldn't sell, but because the tolls made the game not worth it. The dues, the memberships, the desk fees, the paid forms, the whole apparatus of small extractions, plus the indignity of delivering value and then being left to fend for your own fee. Those agents are dammed up behind a wall of tolls. When the tolls come off — all at once, not one at a time — you don't recruit them one by one. You open the dam.

And the two movements feed each other. Every agent who comes over fills the map with more wants, more listings, more life — which draws more of the public — which gives the agents more people to serve — which draws more agents. Two flywheels geared together: the demand and the supply growing each other. That's the shape of something that starts slow and then goes vertical.

Underneath both movements is one idea, and it's the same inversion from the very beginning: **stop making people ask at someone else's door. Bring the market to theirs.** The free listing brings the buyers to the seller's door. The buyer's platform brings the lenders to the buyer's door. The Interconnector brings the whole web of possible moves to a person who only knew their own small piece of it. Over and over, the same correction: home ground, not away game.

## Epilogue: the better rooms

I don't tell the story of the woman at the desk because I'm angry at her. I tell it because that sentence — *"well, you can't"* — is the whole old world in three words. A door held shut by someone who forgot it was ever optional, defending a gate they were taught to call a law.

I'm not going to argue with the gate. I'm building the open door beside it, and the rooms behind it are better: the market that comes to *you*; the listing that costs nothing to show; the deal that gets built instead of merely found; the team that competes to earn you in the open; the plain-language paper you can actually read. None of it asks permission. All of it is just... the way it should have been, once the internet made the old geometry a choice instead of a fact.

Everybody's a buyer eventually. A seller is just a buyer who's moving. We priced our own kids out of the homes we grew up in, and we did it partly by leaving all these gates standing long after they stopped making sense. This isn't revenge on the people who kept the gates. It's the correction. It's the open door.

Come on in. It's free to look.
